Fight for $20

Better wages. Stronger businesses.

The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 2009. Rent, groceries and childcare have not waited 17 years. A $20 floor pays for a life, and it pays for a better business.

Two jobs means two bosses

About 9 million Americans work more than one job. The hours are only part of the cost.

For the worker

  • Two schedules that don't fit. Move one shift and the other job is at risk.
  • Overtime counts job by job. Sixty hours at two employers can pay no overtime.
  • Two sets of rules, cultures and expectations, and either can change without notice.

For the employer

  • You share your people. When schedules collide, someone calls out.
  • Unstable schedules drive people out. Research on retail and food service workers ties them to who quits.
  • One job means full availability, extra shifts and people who stay long enough to get good.

Tips go on top

$20 is the wage. Tips are the customer's choice, on top of it. A tip should thank someone for good service, not cover their rent. We don't want tips banned, and we don't want them counted toward the wage.

The business case

Written from behind the counter

Most of the argument about wages happens between people who have never made payroll. Operators know the real cost of a low wage is rarely on the wage line. It shows up in turnover, training, empty shifts and bad service.

Turnover is the hidden tax

Every person who quits takes their training with them. You pay to recruit, onboard and cover their shifts, and your best people burn out picking up the slack. Higher pay keeps people longer.

Berkeley economist Michael Reich: higher minimums reduce vacancies and turnover rather than jobs.

Stable teams serve better

A crew that has worked together for a year is faster, makes fewer mistakes and knows the regulars. Guests feel the difference before they read the menu.

A floor levels the field

Employers who already pay well get undercut by competitors who don't. A national floor ends that race to the bottom and lets companies compete on product and service.

Workers are customers

Low-wage workers spend what they earn, close to home. More money in their pockets is more money through local registers.

The human case

A full-time job should cover a life

Work should pay for itself

When wages are too low, taxpayers fill the gap through public assistance. A higher floor moves that cost back to where it belongs.

Time is part of the wage

At $7.25, people work two or three jobs to cover rent. That is time away from kids, school and rest. A real wage gives some of it back.

$20 is not a new idea in practice

Several cities and an entire California industry already pay $20 or more. The question is whether workers in the other places get to catch up.

Inflation already did the math

The $15 people fought for a decade ago buys far less today. $20 is what keeping pace looks like.

What it takes

The honest version

Higher wages are not free, and pretending otherwise hurts the case. Here is what the critics say and what it takes to make $20 work.

The concern
Making it work
The Congressional Budget Office estimated a $17 federal minimum could cost about 700,000 jobs on average, while raising pay for millions.
Phase it in over several years so businesses can plan menus, staffing and prices. Index it afterward so it never freezes for 17 years again.
Research on California's $20 fast-food wage is mixed. One study found fewer hours and more kiosks; another found higher pay without job losses.
Watch the data, publish it and adjust the schedule if needed. The goal is a raise people keep, not one that disappears into cut shifts.
$20 in Mississippi is not $20 in San Francisco. Small, rural and independent businesses run on thin margins.
Longer runways for small employers and lower-cost regions. Pair the floor with relief elsewhere, such as payroll tax credits during the phase-in.
Prices go up.
Some will. Owners should say so plainly. Most guests will pay a little more to be served by people who can afford to live nearby.

The first rung

First jobs still matter

The objection

Restaurants are where teenagers and new arrivals learn to show up on time and look a customer in the eye. Raise the floor, weaker places close, and those first jobs go with them.

It's a fair point. That's why we back a starter rate for workers under 18.

Nobody has to hire a beginner

A 16-year-old with no work history is a gamble. At the full adult rate, many employers won't take it.

A starter rate keeps the door open

Under 18, pay a set share of $20 that rises with age, as Australia does. At 18, the full $20.

Training only pays if people stay

A new hire who quits in six weeks learned little and cost you a hiring cycle. Better pay keeps people long enough to learn the job.

The fairness question

Some teens help feed their families, so some say a lower rate is unfair. But no employer has to hire them. A starter rate gets them hired.

How Australia does it

Australia's adult minimum is A$26.44 an hour (July 2026). Workers under 21 get a set share of the adult rate, rising each birthday. In fast food:

Beyond the wage

Policies we back

We back policies that help hard workers and local businesses, whoever proposes them.

No tax on tips

Federal law for 2025 through 2028

What it does
Tipped workers deduct up to $25,000 a year in tips from federal income tax. Social Security and Medicare still apply. Phases out above $150,000 income ($300,000 joint).
For workers
Keep more of every tip.
For employers
Tipped jobs are easier to fill. Tips are reported separately to the IRS.
Where we stand
The employer pays the $20 base. Tips go on top. Workers should keep more of them.

No tax on overtime

Federal law for 2025 through 2028

What it does
Workers deduct the extra half of time-and-a-half from federal income tax, up to $12,500 a year ($25,000 joint). Social Security and Medicare still apply. Same income limits.
For workers
Extra hours pay more.
For employers
Overtime is a better offer when a shift needs covering. Reported separately to the IRS.
Where we stand
It only reaches people who get overtime. Two part-time jobs usually pay none. One $20 job fixes that.

Status checked November 2026. More policies to come.
