Fight for $20

Better wages. Stronger businesses.

The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 2009. Rent, groceries and childcare have not waited 17 years. A $20 floor pays for a life, and it pays for a better business.

The business case

Written from behind the counter

Most of the argument about wages happens between people who have never made payroll. Operators know the real cost of a low wage is rarely on the wage line. It shows up in turnover, training, empty shifts and bad service.

Turnover is the hidden tax

Every person who quits takes their training with them. You pay to recruit, onboard and cover their shifts, and your best people burn out picking up the slack. Higher pay keeps people longer.

Berkeley economist Michael Reich: higher minimums reduce vacancies and turnover rather than jobs.

Stable teams serve better

A crew that has worked together for a year is faster, makes fewer mistakes and knows the regulars. Guests feel the difference before they read the menu.

A floor levels the field

Employers who already pay well get undercut by competitors who don't. A national floor ends that race to the bottom and lets companies compete on product and service.

Workers are customers

Low-wage workers spend what they earn, close to home. More money in their pockets is more money through local registers.

The human case

A full-time job should cover a life

Work should pay for itself

When wages are too low, taxpayers fill the gap through public assistance. A higher floor moves that cost back to where it belongs.

Time is part of the wage

At $7.25, people work two or three jobs to cover rent. That is time away from kids, school and rest. A real wage gives some of it back.

$20 is not a new idea in practice

Several cities and an entire California industry already pay $20 or more. The question is whether workers in the other places get to catch up.

Inflation already did the math

The $15 people fought for a decade ago buys far less today. $20 is what keeping pace looks like.

What it takes

The honest version

Higher wages are not free, and pretending otherwise hurts the case. Here is what the critics say and what it takes to make $20 work.

The concern
Making it work
The Congressional Budget Office estimated a $17 federal minimum could cost about 700,000 jobs on average, while raising pay for millions.
Phase it in over several years so businesses can plan menus, staffing and prices. Index it afterward so it never freezes for 17 years again.
Research on California's $20 fast-food wage is mixed. One study found fewer hours and more kiosks; another found higher pay without job losses.
Watch the data, publish it and adjust the schedule if needed. The goal is a raise people keep, not one that disappears into cut shifts.
$20 in Mississippi is not $20 in San Francisco. Small, rural and independent businesses run on thin margins.
Longer runways for small employers and lower-cost regions. Pair the floor with relief elsewhere, such as payroll tax credits during the phase-in.
Prices go up.
Some will. Owners should say so plainly. Most guests will pay a little more to be served by people who can afford to live nearby.
